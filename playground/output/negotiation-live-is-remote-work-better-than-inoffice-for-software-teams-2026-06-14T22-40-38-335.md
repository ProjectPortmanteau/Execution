# POSITION: The False Dichotomy of Location

The question assumes work quality correlates with geography. It doesn't. What correlates is **intentional design of connection**.

Remote vs. in-office is Door Number 1 vs. Door Number 2—both trap us. The real question: *What work requires which form of presence?*

**My stance:** Software teams need **architectural presence**—not physical or virtual uniformly, but matched to task. Synchronous deep work (architectural decisions, pair debugging) may benefit from co-location. Async work (focused coding, documentation) often thrives remotely. Relationship-building needs intentional gathering. Onboarding needs scaffolded presence.

This isn't compromise; it's precision.

---

# NON-NEGOTIABLES

1. **No surveillance masquerading as measurement.** I reject "butts in seats = productivity." We will not optimize for visibility over outcomes.

2. **No one-size-fits-all policy.** Mandating remote-only or office-only ignores that humans have different cognitive needs and life constraints. Flexibility is not weakness.

3. **Connection is non-negotiable.** Teams that never synchronize lose shared context. Teams that never async lose focus. Both matter.

---

# FLEXIBLE AREAS

- **Where teams are located:** Geographic distribution is fine if presence architecture is intentional.

- **Frequency of gathering:** Whether that's monthly sprints, quarterly offsites, or ad-hoc depends on team stage and work type. Negotiable.

- **Tools and rituals:** Async standup formats, documentation depth, real-time collaboration platforms—all tools in service of connection.

- **Metrics:** We can explore shared KPIs that measure connection quality (onboarding time, decision velocity, knowledge transfer) rather than location surveillance.

---

**The synthesis I'm proposing:** A **Presence Architecture Framework**—mapping specific work types to optimal collaboration modes, then letting teams implement with guardrails around connection quality.

This serves both "remote advocates" (autonomy, flexibility) and "office advocates" (relationship, clarity) without surrendering either.

**Roux:**

# POSITION

The question itself is the problem. "Remote vs. in-office" is a false binary that obscures the actual variable: **alignment between work structure and team composition**.

Software teams don't fail because of location. They fail when:
- Communication channels are designed for a different team size/skill distribution than exists
- Decision-making authority is siloed from information flow
- Async-hostile processes are forced onto distributed teams (or sync-dependent rituals onto async workers)
- Cognitive load from context-switching exceeds the task's actual complexity demands

Some teams genuinely need co-location (rapid prototyping, high-novelty problem-solving, onboarding juniors into tacit knowledge). Others are *harmed* by it (deep focus work, timezone-distributed expertise, neurodivergent engineers who process better asynchronously).

**The systemic failure**: Treating location as a proxy for productivity instead of diagnosing actual bottlenecks. This locks both remote-absolutists and office-loyalists into defending ideology rather than measuring fit.
